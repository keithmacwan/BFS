# Are Critical Laboratory Alarms Reaching the Right Person at the Right Time?

## A Bio Facility Solutions Guide to Detection, Notification, Escalation and Response

**Bio Facility Solutions | Engineering + Laboratory Science**

A critical alarm is only useful when the correct condition is detected, classified appropriately, transmitted reliably, delivered to the right people, acknowledged, escalated when necessary and followed by a clear response.

## The BFS Alarm Chain

1. **Detect** — Is the correct laboratory condition actually being measured?
2. **Classify** — Do the alarm limit, priority, delay and deadband match the consequence?
3. **Communicate** — Can the event travel through controller, network, server and notification systems?
4. **Route** — Is the alarm going to the correct person or team for the site, system and time of day?
5. **Acknowledge** — Do you know that a human has taken ownership?
6. **Escalate** — What happens when the first recipient does not respond?
7. **Respond** — Does the message give the responder enough information to act quickly?

## How critical alarm strategies fail

- Alarm thresholds or delays do not reflect actual laboratory risk.
- A BAS server, network, gateway, SMTP, SMS or notification-service failure interrupts delivery.
- Notification groups contain outdated contacts or unclear ownership.
- A message is delivered after hours but no acknowledgement or escalation is required.
- Frequent nuisance alarms reduce attention to important events.
- Point names do not clearly identify the room, asset or condition.
- A common-mode power or network failure affects both the protected asset and its monitoring path.
- Large system events create an alarm flood that obscures the initiating failure.

## 12 questions every facility should ask about critical laboratory alarms

1. Which laboratory alarms are truly critical, and why?
2. Are alarm names and locations understandable without opening drawings?
3. Are limits, delays and deadbands based on actual operational risk?
4. Who receives each critical alarm during business hours?
5. Who receives it after hours, weekends and holidays?
6. How is acknowledgement confirmed?
7. How long before an unacknowledged alarm escalates?
8. What happens if the BAS server, network or notification service is unavailable?
9. Are alarm contacts and notification groups routinely reviewed?
10. Do nuisance alarms make important alarms easier to miss?
11. Does the message tell the responder what to check and who to call?
12. Has the complete alarm path been tested recently?

## A BFS critical alarm assessment can include

- Critical alarm inventory and classification
- Point descriptions and naming review
- Priority, threshold, delay and deadband review
- Notification groups and after-hours routing
- Acknowledgement and escalation strategy
- BAS/network/server/communications dependency review
- Loss-of-monitoring and common-mode failure scenarios
- Operator response instructions and ownership
- Nuisance-alarm and alarm-flood observations
- Critical Alarm Matrix with recommended next steps

## Important context

This guide is an infrastructure-risk and alarm-strategy discussion tool. It does not prescribe universal alarm limits, notification times or response procedures. Criticality, thresholds, delays, escalation timing and operator actions should be established for the specific laboratory process, equipment, validated requirements, institutional policies and applicable codes or standards.
