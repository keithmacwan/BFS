## Protect the systems your science depends on

Laboratories rely on tightly connected building systems to maintain temperature, humidity, ventilation, pressure relationships, power quality, cold-storage conditions and alarm visibility. A weakness in any one of these systems can place high-value samples, specialized equipment, research schedules and operational continuity at risk.

The challenge is rarely the absence of equipment. It is often the gap between systems:

- A freezer may be connected to emergency power, but the upstream distribution may contain an unrecognized single point of failure.
- A room may be monitored by the building automation system, but the alarm delay, priority or escalation path may not reflect the actual scientific risk.
- An HVAC system may meet normal operating requirements, but its failure response may depend on undocumented manual actions.
- A critical alarm may reach a control room, yet no one may have a clear response procedure, contact list or recovery time objective.

Bio Facility Solutions helps organizations find these vulnerabilities before they become operational events.

## Three outcomes of a laboratory infrastructure risk assessment

### 01 — Protect critical research assets

Identify infrastructure conditions that could expose samples, reagents, cell cultures, controlled environments and specialized laboratory equipment to unacceptable temperature, humidity, pressure or power excursions.

### 02 — Strengthen operational resilience

Understand how systems respond during equipment failure, utility interruption, alarm conditions and other abnormal events—and whether staff have the information, authority and time required to act.

### 03 — Support defensible capital decisions

Translate technical observations into a prioritized risk register and implementation roadmap so leadership can distinguish immediate corrective actions from medium- and long-term capital needs.

## Executive summary

Many facilities invest heavily in research equipment while the infrastructure supporting that equipment receives less integrated review. Mechanical, electrical, controls, monitoring and operational procedures may be designed or managed by different groups. Each component may appear acceptable on its own, while important dependencies remain untested or undocumented.

A Laboratory Infrastructure Risk Assessment examines the full chain between the building system and the scientific outcome. It asks:

1. **What must remain available?**  
   Which rooms, equipment, samples, processes and utilities are critical?

2. **What could interrupt them?**  
   Which failures, dependencies, capacity limitations or response gaps could cause loss of control?

3. **How would the organization know?**  
   Are the right conditions monitored, prioritized, routed and escalated to the right people?

4. **How would the organization respond?**  
   Are responsibilities, response procedures, recovery actions and communication paths clear?

5. **What should be addressed first?**  
   Which risks require immediate mitigation, operational improvement, further engineering study or capital investment?

The result is not simply a list of deficiencies. It is a practical decision framework that connects engineering risk to laboratory operations.

# The BFS Risk Framework

## Layer 1 — Asset and process criticality

The assessment begins with the science. BFS works with laboratory and facility stakeholders to understand what is being protected and why it matters.

Typical considerations include:

- Sample or product sensitivity
- Acceptable environmental limits
- Maximum tolerable interruption
- Recovery difficulty and replacement lead time
- Equipment and research value
- Operational, schedule and funding consequences
- Availability of backup capacity or alternate space

This prevents every issue from being treated as equally important. A risk becomes meaningful when it is connected to a specific scientific or operational consequence.

## Layer 2 — Infrastructure dependency and failure exposure

Once critical assets and processes are understood, BFS reviews the infrastructure chain supporting them.

### HVAC and environmental control

- Temperature and relative-humidity control
- Ventilation and air-change performance
- Room pressure relationships
- Heating, cooling, humidification and dehumidification dependencies
- Control sequences and failure modes
- Redundancy, capacity and recovery limitations
- Impact of planned shutdowns and seasonal conditions

### Emergency power and electrical resilience

- Critical equipment power sources
- Emergency or standby power coverage
- Distribution-path dependencies
- Transfer arrangements and restoration sequence
- Single points of failure
- Capacity and coordination concerns requiring further study
- Testing, maintenance and operational readiness

### Cold storage and monitoring

- Ultra-low-temperature freezers, refrigerators, cold rooms and incubators
- Power and environmental dependencies
- Local and remote monitoring
- Alarm thresholds, delays and escalation
- Backup storage capacity
- Emergency relocation and recovery planning
- Equipment inventory and criticality classification

### BAS and critical alarms

- Monitored points and point descriptions
- Alarm limits, priorities and time delays
- Routing, notification and escalation
- Alarm acknowledgement and response expectations
- Communication failures and loss-of-monitoring scenarios
- Nuisance alarms, gaps and inconsistent naming
- Operator instructions and ownership

### Critical utilities and facility resilience

- Chilled water, heating water, steam and process utilities
- Domestic or purified-water dependencies
- Drainage, leakage and flood exposure
- Medical or specialty gases, where applicable
- Maintainability, isolation and shutdown planning
- Documentation, labelling and emergency access

## Layer 3 — Detection, response and recovery

Reliable equipment is only one part of resilience. BFS examines whether a developing condition can be detected early enough and whether the organization can respond before the impact becomes irreversible.

The review considers:

- Who receives the first notification?
- Is the message clear enough to support action?
- What happens if the first recipient does not respond?
- Are after-hours responsibilities defined?
- Can responders find the correct room, asset, valve, panel or control point?
- Are temporary measures available?
- Is there a documented escalation and communication path?
- Has the response been tested through drills or scenarios?

# How risks are prioritized

Each finding is evaluated using a practical combination of consequence, likelihood, detectability and recovery readiness.

| Priority | Typical meaning | Recommended response |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | A credible failure could cause severe or irreversible impact, with inadequate detection, backup or response capability. | Immediate risk controls and executive visibility. |
| **High** | Significant operational or research impact is possible and current safeguards may be insufficient. | Near-term corrective action, engineering review or funded project. |
| **Moderate** | The risk is manageable but resilience, documentation or response capability should be improved. | Planned operational improvement or capital work. |
| **Low** | Limited consequence or strong existing controls; improvement would strengthen good practice. | Track, monitor or incorporate into routine planning. |

Risk priority is not based on equipment age alone. A newer system can still present a high risk if its dependencies, alarms or response arrangements are weak. An older system may remain manageable when redundancy, maintenance and recovery measures are strong.

# The assessment process

## 01 — Discover

Define the facility, laboratory functions, critical assets, known concerns and business objectives. Identify the people who understand the science, infrastructure and operational response.

**Typical inputs**

- Facility and laboratory overview
- Critical equipment inventory
- Available drawings and specifications
- BAS and alarm information
- Incident and excursion history
- Maintenance and testing records
- Existing procedures and capital plans

## 02 — Assess

Review available documentation, interview stakeholders and conduct a targeted site walkdown. Trace critical systems from source to end use and identify failure modes, dependencies and response gaps.

## 03 — Prioritize

Evaluate findings based on scientific consequence, operational impact, likelihood, detection capability, redundancy and recovery readiness.

## 04 — Act

Provide a practical roadmap that separates immediate operational controls from engineering studies, maintenance actions and longer-term capital projects.

# What the client receives

The scope can be tailored to the facility, but a typical engagement may include:

- **Executive summary** — the most important risks, decisions and recommended actions in leadership language
- **Laboratory infrastructure risk register** — findings, consequences, existing safeguards, risk priority and recommended next steps
- **System observations** — structured review of HVAC, emergency power, cold storage, BAS, critical alarms and relevant utilities
- **Immediate-action list** — practical measures that can reduce exposure before larger projects are completed
- **Capital-planning roadmap** — near-, medium- and long-term recommendations organized for budget discussion
- **Documentation-gap list** — missing drawings, sequences, inventories, procedures or ownership information
- **Optional alarm matrix** — point, condition, priority, delay, recipient, escalation and operator-response information
- **Optional response framework** — defined actions for selected equipment failures, excursions or utility interruptions

Where detailed design, testing or code analysis is required, BFS can define the next engineering step and the information needed to move forward.

# Common warning signs

Your facility may benefit from an assessment if:

- Critical freezers or laboratory equipment have unclear emergency-power coverage.
- Alarm notifications are inconsistent, delayed or dependent on a single person.
- Staff receive frequent nuisance alarms and may miss a meaningful event.
- Critical room conditions are monitored, but response instructions are unclear.
- Drawings, equipment inventories or system sequences do not reflect current conditions.
- Backup storage exists, but transfer capacity and relocation procedures have not been confirmed.
- Laboratory expansion or new equipment is planned without a coordinated infrastructure-capacity review.
- The organization has experienced a temperature, humidity, pressure, power or water-related event.
- Capital needs are known informally but have not been translated into a prioritized risk case.
- Facility and laboratory teams understand different parts of the risk, but no one has reviewed the complete chain.
