Critical Alarm Strategy Guide

Are critical laboratory alarms reaching the right person at the right time?

A practical guide to the complete alarm chain, from detection to action.

A critical alarm is only useful when the correct condition is detected, classified appropriately, transmitted reliably, delivered to the right people, acknowledged, escalated when necessary and followed by a clear response.

The hidden risk

An alarm can exist in the BAS and still fail operationally.

Laboratories often have thousands of monitored points across HVAC systems, freezers, cold rooms, pressure-controlled spaces, emergency power, utilities and specialty equipment. The presence of an alarm point does not prove that the alarm will protect the laboratory.

  • The sensor may detect the condition, but the alarm limit or delay may not reflect the actual laboratory risk.
  • The BAS may create a critical alarm, while a server, network, email or notification failure prevents delivery.
  • The message may reach someone, but not identify the room, asset, condition or expected response clearly enough.
  • The first recipient may be unavailable after hours, with no defined acknowledgement or escalation path.
  • Frequent nuisance alarms may reduce attention to the event that actually matters.

BFS reviews the alarm as an end-to-end operational system, not only as a BAS point.

Three outcomes

A stronger alarm strategy improves visibility, response and accountability.

01

Detect meaningful conditions

Confirm that critical points, alarm thresholds, delays, deadbands and priorities match the consequence to the laboratory rather than simply a default controls setting.

02

Reach the right responder

Define who receives each alarm during normal hours and after hours, how delivery is verified and what happens when the first recipient does not respond.

03

Turn alarms into action

Give responders enough information to locate the asset, understand the condition, protect the laboratory and escalate to facilities, laboratory leadership or service support.

The BFS Alarm Chain

Seven links between a developing condition and an effective response.

A weakness anywhere in the chain can make a technically functioning alarm operationally ineffective.

01

Detect

Is the correct condition actually being measured?

Review sensor coverage, monitored points, point descriptions, location, signal quality and whether the monitored variable reflects the laboratory risk.

TemperatureHumidityPressureEquipment statusPower status
02

Classify

Does the alarm logic match the consequence?

Review limits, priority, delay, deadband, persistence, suppression and return-to-normal logic. Alarm settings should balance early warning with nuisance-alarm control.

03

Communicate

Can the alarm travel through the full technology path?

Trace the route through controller, network, server, gateway, email/SMS/notification service and recipient device. Identify dependencies on power, IT infrastructure and third-party services.

04

Route

Is it going to the correct person or team?

Confirm notification groups by site, system, alarm category, time of day and operating responsibility. Keep contact lists and ownership current.

05

Acknowledge

Do you know that a human has taken ownership?

Receipt and acknowledgement are not the same thing. Define how alarm ownership is confirmed, recorded and handed over during shift changes or prolonged events.

06

Escalate

What happens when the first response does not occur?

Define time-based escalation to the next responder, supervisor, facilities team or laboratory contact based on severity and expected response time.

07

Respond

Can the responder take the right action quickly?

Provide clear location, asset identification, alarm condition, immediate action, contact information and escalation expectations. The message should support action without requiring detective work.

Failure Scenarios

How a critical alarm can fail even when the equipment is monitored.

Wrong logic

The condition occurs, but the alarm comes too late.

A delay, threshold or deadband selected for convenience can consume valuable response time.

Communications loss

The BAS sees the event, but the recipient never does.

Network, server, gateway, SMTP, SMS or notification-service failure can interrupt the alarm path.

Stale routing

The message is sent to a person who is no longer responsible.

Contact groups can become outdated after staffing, coverage or organizational changes.

No escalation

The first recipient misses the alarm after hours.

Without acknowledgement and escalation, delivery can be mistaken for response.

Nuisance overload

Frequent alarms reduce attention to critical events.

Poor prioritization, unstable points and duplicate notifications can create alarm fatigue.

Unclear message

The responder receives an alarm but cannot act quickly.

Generic point names, missing locations or no response instruction can delay intervention.

Common-mode failure

The event also disables the monitoring path.

A power or network outage may affect both the protected equipment and the system expected to report its failure.

Alarm flood

A major failure produces dozens or hundreds of alarms at once.

Without hierarchy and event grouping, responders may struggle to identify the initiating event and highest-consequence conditions.

Alarm Review Method

From point list to operational response.

01

Inventory

Identify critical rooms, equipment, utilities and BAS/alarm points. Capture point name, tag, condition, priority, delay, limits and current recipients.

02

Challenge

Ask what happens during credible HVAC, power, cold-storage, utility, controls, network and server failures, and determine whether the alarm survives the event.

03

Test

Where permitted by the facility, verify selected alarm paths from field condition or simulated point through notification, acknowledgement and escalation.

04

Standardize

Create consistent naming, priority, delay, routing, escalation and response expectations tied to laboratory consequence.

12 questions to ask now

Can you answer these confidently?

Which laboratory alarms are truly critical, and why?
Are alarm names and locations understandable without opening drawings?
Are limits, delays and deadbands based on actual operational risk?
Who receives each critical alarm during business hours?
Who receives it after hours, weekends and holidays?
How is acknowledgement confirmed?
How long before an unacknowledged alarm escalates?
What happens if the BAS server, network or notification service is unavailable?
Are alarm contacts and notification groups routinely reviewed?
Do nuisance alarms make important alarms easier to miss?
Does the message tell the responder what to check and who to call?
Has the complete alarm path been tested recently?

Important context

Alarm settings must be facility-specific.

This guide is an infrastructure-risk and alarm-strategy discussion tool. It does not prescribe universal alarm limits, notification times or response procedures. Criticality, thresholds, delays, escalation timing and operator actions should be established for the specific laboratory process, equipment, validated requirements, institutional policies and applicable codes or standards.

Alarm testing should be planned and authorized so that simulations, overrides or notification tests do not create unintended operational impacts.
